SYSTEM OVERVIEW
Why does RocksDB need a Write-Ahead Log?
01
LSM-Tree Architecture
Writes first land in the volatile in-memory MemTable (a lock-free skip-list). Background compaction periodically flushes sorted runs to disk as SST files. Optimized for write throughput at the cost of read amplification.
02
The Durability Problem
RAM is ephemeral by nature. A power failure or kernel panic before MemTable flush causes total data loss for all recent writes. SST files on disk are safe, but anything in-memory is gone.
03
WAL — The Solution
Every write operation (Put, Merge, Delete) is appended sequentially to the WAL on disk BEFORE MemTable insertion. On recovery, the engine replays the log to reconstruct exact MemTable state.
KEY GUARANTEE:  WAL replay reconstructs MemTable exactly as it was — zero data loss for any acknowledged write.

RECOVERY PATH
On crash-restart, log::Reader replays every WAL record sequentially. Each 7-byte header carries a CRC-32C checksum. Any mismatch signals a torn write and the record is discarded — the database never enters an inconsistent state. Recovery time scales proportionally with uncompressed WAL volume (Study 5).

EXPERIMENT  ·  STUDY 1
The Performance Tax of Durability
How much does fsync() actually cost?
WHAT WE DID
Modified db_impl_write.cc to inject atomic counters per sync mode. Ran the same 10,000-write workload under three policies: No WAL, OS-buffered, and Strict fsync.
HYPOTHESIS
Strict fsync would be slower due to the kernel flush overhead. We expected a 10–50× gap, not the extreme result we found.
KEY INSIGHT
fsync() introduces a 524× throughput floor — not a gradual cost, but a cliff. Every production engineer must choose their durability tier deliberately.

--- ppt/slides/slide6.xml ---
EXPERIMENT  ·  STUDY 2
Header Overhead & Block Fragmentation
What is the storage cost of fixed-block alignment?
WHAT WE DID
Instrumented log_writer.cc with two atomic counters tracking header bytes emitted vs. payload bytes written across 5,000 sequential records of 10 KB each.
HYPOTHESIS
Fixed 32 KB block boundaries would force some records to be split, producing measurable header-to-payload overhead — but the system is designed to keep it negligible.
KEY INSIGHT
Only 0.1% overhead from 7-byte per-record headers — hardware-page alignment is effectively free, but intentional.

--- ppt/slides/slide7.xml ---
EXPERIMENT  ·  STUDY 3
WALRecoveryMode Comparison
Which recovery mode minimises MTTR?
WHAT WE DID
Modified db_impl_open.cc to time the full recovery phase under three WALRecoveryMode settings: AbsoluteConsistency, PointInTime, and TolerateCorruptedTailRecords.
HYPOTHESIS
Stricter consistency modes would do more validation work per record, producing measurably longer recovery times even on a clean-shutdown WAL.
KEY INSIGHT
TolerateCorruptedTailRecords is 1.5× faster to recover — half the MTTR for workloads that can tolerate tail-record loss.

EXPERIMENT  ·  STUDY 4
Concurrent Writing & Group Commit Efficiency
Does concurrency actually help when fsync is the bottleneck?
WHAT WE DID
Instrumented write_thread.cc to track group-commit batch size and total ops/sec as concurrent writer threads scaled from 1 → 4 → 8. Same total write volume per run.
HYPOTHESIS
Even though every batch still incurs one fsync, amortizing that cost over more writes per batch should produce super-linear throughput gains.
KEY INSIGHT
Group Commit delivers 4.3× throughput amplification at 8 threads — the leader-follower pattern is the WAL's most powerful performance mechanism.
KEY INSIGHT

EXPERIMENT  ·  STUDY 5
MTTR vs. WAL Volume — Recovery Scaling
How does recovery time scale with WAL data volume?
WHAT WE DID
Used db_impl_open.cc telemetry to measure full WAL replay time as uncompressed log size grew from 10 K to 500 K records. Each point is the average of three cold-start runs.
HYPOTHESIS
WAL replay is sequential and compute-bound per record. Recovery time should scale sub-linearly due to buffered I/O — or linearly if CPU parsing dominates.
KEY INSIGHT
MTTR scales proportionally with WAL volume — stalled compaction is a direct availability risk. WAL rotation (SwitchWAL) must be tuned to cap recovery window.

CONCLUSION
The RocksDB WAL is a precision-engineered tradeoff engine.
Sequential I/O and fixed-block alignment give the WAL near-optimal write throughput. Group Commit amortizes the kernel-boundary cost of fsync across concurrent workloads — turning concurrency from a problem into a performance multiplier. CRC-32C torn-write detection makes the log the single source of truth after any failure. The fundamental lesson: every parameter — sync policy, block size, recovery mode, rotation interval — is a dial that engineers must tune consciously against their availability and durability SLA.
